% ABSTRACT
% =========================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Wrist-worn continuous stress monitoring systems offer a non-invasive tool for proactive health management. However, photoplethysmography (BVP) and electrodermal activity (EDA) sensors placed at the wrist are susceptible to movement artifacts. Routine physical activity (walking, desk work, arm articulation) naturally activates the sympathetic nervous system, increasing heart rate and sudomotor sweating in a manner that closely resembles physiological responses to mental stress. Standard machine learning classifiers forced to make predictions on 100\% of data windows (Full Coverage) produce high rates of motion-induced false alarms, contributing directly to alarm fatigue. In this paper, we propose a reliable stress detection framework integrating Isotonic probability calibration with selective classification augmented by kinematic context on the benchmark WESAD dataset ($N=15$). The framework allows the classifier to abstain from making a decision (assigning an \textit{Uncertain} label) when posterior confidence falls below a safety threshold. Systematic benchmarks across eight model architectures (seven tabular classifiers and a multi-layer perceptron) under a 15-fold Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) protocol with inner subject-grouped cross-validation show that motion vulnerability is common across all models ($\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}} \approx 12.2\% - 24.0\%$). The proposed rejection rule suppresses between 55.0\% and 90.0\% of these motion-induced false alarms across benchmarks. At the optimal confidence threshold $\tau = 0.80$ using XGBoost, the framework achieves a Selective Macro $F_1$-score of \textbf{95.20\%} (Stress $F_1 = 93.15\%$, 95\% CI: [84.80\%, 98.54\%]), reduces Expected Calibration Error (ECE) to \textbf{0.0290}, and limits $\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}}$ to \textbf{4.07\%} (95\% CI: [0.0\%, 11.07\%]) while maintaining $69.42\%$ usable coverage (95\% CI: [58.34\%, 79.84\%]). Fold-by-fold breakdowns across the 15 WESAD subjects, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, tabular-versus-neural comparisons, and TreeSHAP feature attributions demonstrate empirical consistency and examine individual autonomic dissociation on an outlier hypo-responder subject (S14).
\end{abstract}

% I. INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}
Prolonged psychological stress is a recognized risk factor for immune dysfunction, metabolic disorders, and cardiovascular complications \cite{cohen2007psychological, schneiderman2005stress, can2019stress}. The widespread availability of consumer smartbands and smartwatches allows continuous, non-invasive tracking of physiological parameters in daily life \cite{gjoreski2016continuous, schmidt2018wesad, sano2018multimodal}.

However, practical deployment in ambulatory environments faces a persistent obstacle: \textit{motion-induced false alarms} \cite{schumm2010effect, tamura2014wearable, zhang2014troika}. Physical movement stimulates the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) to support metabolic demands, causing peripheral vasoconstriction, elevated pulse rate, and eccrine sweat secretion on the palmar and wrist surfaces \cite{boucsein2012electrodermal}. When recorded by optical blood volume pulse (BVP) and galvanic skin conductance (EDA) sensors, these physiological adjustments closely resemble the autonomic changes induced by acute psychological stress.

In existing studies on the standard WESAD dataset \cite{schmidt2018wesad, siirtola2019continuous, bobade2020stress, dalmeida2021computer, behinaein2022transformer, bottcher2023detection}, models are commonly evaluated under a forced-prediction scheme requiring binary classifications ($\text{Stress}$ vs. $\text{Non-Stress}$) over 100\% of temporal windows (Full Coverage). During physical activity, both neural networks and standard classifiers often output overconfident erroneous predictions \cite{guo2017calibration}. The resulting high frequency of false warnings during routine physical movement causes alarm fatigue, leading users to disable alerts or stop wearing the monitor.

To address this challenge, our work applies the principles of \textbf{Trustworthy Machine Learning}: rather than forcing predictions on all inputs, an ambulatory monitoring system should quantify its predictive uncertainty and withhold decisions on ambiguous or motion-corrupted samples through selective classification \cite{chow1970optimum, cortes2016learning, geifman2017selective, geifman2019selectivenet, hendrickx2021machine}.

\subsection{Scientific Contributions}
This paper presents four main contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Formalization and Quantification of Motion Vulnerability ($\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}}$):} We define the $\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}}$ metric to isolate model behavior during active motion and benchmark eight distinct algorithms (seven tabular models and a multi-layer perceptron) under 15-fold Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation \cite{saeb2017need, little2019using}. We show that motion false alarm vulnerability affects all model families ($\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}} \approx 12.2\% - 24.0\%$) when full coverage is enforced.
    \item \textbf{Dual-level Group-aware Calibration Pipeline:} We design an Isotonic probability calibration protocol nested inside an inner subject-stratified cross-validation (\texttt{GroupKFold}). This structure prevents data leakage arising from 91.7\% sliding-window overlap, aligning model posterior confidence with observed empirical frequencies ($\text{ECE} = 0.0290$).
    \item \textbf{Selective Decision Framework with Kinematic Context Integration:} We formulate an abstention rule based on confidence threshold $\tau$ that leverages tri-axial accelerometry (ACC) features. The framework reduces motion-induced false alarms by 55.0\% to 90.0\% across all eight evaluated models, enabling calibrated XGBoost to reach a Selective Macro $F_1$ of 95.20\% at 69.42\% coverage, validated by multi-seed tests (5 seeds) and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.
    \item \textbf{Biomedical XAI Attribution and Tabular-vs-Deep Empirical Analysis:} Using TreeSHAP \cite{lundberg2017unified}, we quantify the relative contributions of four sensor modalities and identify the conflicting feature patterns that trigger model abstention. We provide a controlled empirical comparison between tree ensembles and neural models on the same standardized feature space, analyzing an outlier participant with muted electrodermal response (Subject S14) to inform future personalized architectures.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work \& Research Gap Analysis}

\subsection{Wrist-Based Wearable Stress Detection}
Early ambulatory stress monitoring focused primarily on chest-worn straps or multi-lead electrocardiography (ECG) \cite{taskforce1996heart}. With the introduction of multi-sensor wrist devices such as the Empatica E4 \cite{garbarino2014empatica}, recent studies have emphasized optical BVP, EDA, skin temperature (TEMP), and tri-axial accelerometry (ACC) \cite{can2019stress, gjoreski2016continuous}.

Schmidt et al. \cite{schmidt2018wesad} established the WESAD benchmark ($N=15$), capturing synchronized chest and wrist data. Their findings indicated that wrist-based classification is considerably more challenging than chest-based monitoring due to unrestricted arm movement. Bobade and Vani \cite{bobade2020stress} examined classical classifiers and deep CNN/LSTM architectures, achieving 75\%--84\% accuracy on wrist data; however, their evaluation used random K-Fold splitting, which causes temporal data leakage between overlapping windows. Siirtola \cite{siirtola2019continuous} extracted heart rate variability (HRV) metrics from wrist BVP, noting that individual physiological differences present substantial hurdles for cross-subject generalization. Can et al. \cite{can2019stress}, Sano et al. \cite{sano2018multimodal}, and Böttcher et al. \cite{bottcher2023detection} corroborated that physiological heterogeneity degrades accuracy in real-world settings. Recent works by Behinaein et al. \cite{behinaein2022transformer} and Dalmeida and Masala \cite{dalmeida2021computer} investigated deep transformer and vision-based architectures, though computational demands on embedded hardware remain an open constraint.

\subsection{Motion Artifacts and False Alarms}
Arm and wrist movements distort optical BVP waveforms and change skin-electrode contact geometry \cite{tamura2014wearable, zhang2014troika, schumm2010effect}. In addition to physical displacement artifacts, physical activity stimulates metabolic and autonomic adjustments: heart rate increases and eccrine sweat glands activate for thermoregulation \cite{boucsein2012electrodermal, schumm2010effect}.

Prior research has approached motion primarily through signal processing methods, such as Butterworth filtering or adaptive artifact cancellation \cite{zhang2014troika}. While frequency filtering removes high-frequency electrical and optical noise, it cannot alter authentic physiological tachycardia and sweat production caused by exercise. Consequently, when classifiers are required to predict on all windows, false alarm rates remain high during physical activities, leading to alarm fatigue \cite{can2019stress}.

\subsection{Selective Classification and Learning with Rejection}
Originating with Chow's formulation \cite{chow1970optimum}, learning with rejection allows a classifier to withhold judgment when the expected cost of an error exceeds the cost of abstention. Geifman and El-Yaniv \cite{geifman2017selective, geifman2019selectivenet} adapted selective classification to deep neural networks, analyzing the risk-coverage trade-off. Additional theoretical work by Cortes et al. \cite{cortes2016learning}, Grandvalet et al. \cite{grandvalet2002adaptive}, Wiqvist et al. \cite{wiqvist2022partially}, and a survey by Hendrickx et al. \cite{hendrickx2021machine} examined rejection frameworks in risk-sensitive applications.

Despite these developments, selective classification has not been widely utilized for wearable stress tracking. Our work applies confidence thresholding $\tau$ informed by kinematic context to govern selective abstention, specifically mitigating motion-induced false alarms.

\subsection{Probability Calibration and Windowing Leakage}
Effective selective classification requires well-calibrated posterior probabilities: the predicted confidence should reflect the empirical probability of correctness \cite{guo2017calibration}. Uncalibrated models often produce overconfident outputs. Standard post-processing calibration techniques include Platt scaling (Sigmoid) \cite{platt1999probabilistic}, Beta calibration \cite{kull2017beta}, and non-parametric Isotonic regression \cite{zadrozny2002transforming, niculescu2005predicting}.

In physiological time series, sliding windows (e.g., 60-second windows with 5-second shifts) introduce 91.7\% overlap between consecutive observations. Applying conventional random cross-validation allows contiguous windows from the same subject to appear in both training and calibration splits, introducing optimistic bias in calibration metrics \cite{saeb2017need, little2019using}. We address this via a two-level evaluation framework with subject-grouped inner splits.

\subsection{Rationale for Tabular Machine Learning}
Given the prevalence of deep neural networks, our focus on decision-tree ensembles (Random Forest, LightGBM, XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}) is based on three considerations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample Size Constraints ($N=15$ Subjects):} WESAD contains 15 participants. Studies by Shwartz-Ziv and Armon \cite{shwartzziv2022tabular}, Grinsztajn et al. \cite{grinsztajn2022why}, and Borisov et al. \cite{borisov2024deep} show that on small-to-medium tabular datasets, tree-based models frequently match or exceed deep neural networks. High-capacity neural networks can overfit subject-specific physiological baselines on small cohorts, impairing leave-one-subject-out generalization.
    \item \textbf{Biomedical Interpretability (XAI):} Standard HRV and EDA metrics (RMSSD, LF/HF, SCR peaks) are grounded in established autonomic physiology \cite{taskforce1996heart, greco2016cvxeda, tarvainen2014kubios}. Combined with TreeSHAP \cite{lundberg2017unified}, tree ensembles allow feature attribution for individual predictions and abstentions.
    \item \textbf{Embedded Hardware Feasibility:} Commercial wrist instruments operate under strict micro-controller power budgets. Decision trees paired with Isotonic look-up tables require low memory and $O(\text{depth})$ inference complexity, facilitating on-device execution.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proposed Methodology}

\subsection{Preprocessing and Feature Extraction}
Continuous recordings from the Empatica E4 wristband are partitioned into rolling windows of $W = 60$\,s with step size $\Delta t = 5$\,s (91.7\% overlap). A 60-second window length satisfies standard recommendations for short-term HRV estimation \cite{taskforce1996heart}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BVP Signal (64\,Hz):} Filtered via a 2nd-order Butterworth bandpass filter (0.5--8.0\,Hz) to reduce baseline wander. Systolic peaks are identified to compute the inter-beat interval (IBI) sequence. Spurious intervals outside 300--1800\,ms are removed, and the resulting series is resampled to 4\,Hz via cubic spline interpolation \cite{tarvainen2014kubios}. Extracted metrics include time-domain indicators (Mean HR, SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50) and frequency-domain components via Welch's method: low-frequency power LF (0.04--0.15\,Hz), high-frequency power HF (0.15--0.40\,Hz), and the sympathovagal balance ratio LF/HF.
    \item \textbf{EDA Signal (4\,Hz):} Decomposed using cvxEDA \cite{greco2016cvxeda} into tonic skin conductance levels (SCL) and phasic skin conductance responses (SCR). Features include SCR peak count, maximum peak amplitude, mean and variance of both tonic and phasic components, and phasic energy $\sum \text{Phasic}^2$ \cite{boucsein2012electrodermal}.
    \item \textbf{TEMP Signal (4\,Hz):} Peripheral skin temperature tracks vasoconstriction. Extracted features include mean, standard deviation, extrema, and linear regression slope.
    \item \textbf{ACC Signal (32\,Hz):} Tri-axial acceleration values are combined into the Euclidean norm $a(t) = \sqrt{a_x^2(t) + a_y^2(t) + a_z^2(t)}$. Features include mean, variance $\text{Var}(\text{ACC})$ (proxy for body movement intensity), peak value, zero-crossing rate (ZCR), and spectral energy.
\end{itemize}

The final dataset comprises 5,294 observation windows across all 15 valid participants from WESAD ($S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, S10, S11, S13, S14, S15, S16, S17$; noting that $S1$ was an initial protocol test and $S12$ was excluded by the dataset creators due to hardware sensor malfunction \cite{schmidt2018wesad}). These 15 participants are evaluated across 15 independent test folds. The standardized feature space consists of 25 continuous features.

\subsection{Probability Calibration}
Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represent the feature vector and $y \in \{0, 1\}$ denote the binary ground truth (0: Non-Stress, 1: Stress). The raw model score $f(x)$ is converted to a calibrated probability via monotonic Isotonic regression \cite{zadrozny2002transforming, niculescu2005predicting}:
\begin{equation}
\hat{P}(Y=1|x) = g(f(x))
\end{equation}
where $g(\cdot)$ minimizes squared error subject to non-decreasing monotonicity: $\min_g \sum (y_i - g(f(x_i)))^2$.

Calibration error is evaluated using Expected Calibration Error (ECE) \cite{guo2017calibration} computed over $M=10$ equal-width bins:
\begin{equation}
\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{N} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|
\end{equation}
where $\text{acc}(B_m)$ and $\text{conf}(B_m)$ denote empirical accuracy and average confidence in bin $B_m$, respectively.

\subsection{Selective Decision Rule}
Let $\hat{p}_1 = \hat{P}(Y=1|x)$. We define the selective classifier $(h, g_\tau)$ with an abstention option governed by confidence threshold $\tau \in [0.5, 1.0)$ \cite{chow1970optimum, geifman2017selective}:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}(x) = 
\begin{cases} 
1 \; (\text{Stress}), & \text{if } \hat{p}_1 \ge \tau \\
0 \; (\text{Non-Stress}), & \text{if } (1 - \hat{p}_1) \ge \tau \\
\text{Uncertain} \; (\text{Abstain}), & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Rather than applying a separate heuristic cutoff on acceleration values, our architecture feeds the 6 kinematic features directly into the model alongside the 19 physiological features. Gradient boosted trees learn non-linear relationships between movement patterns and autonomic responses. When intense movement causes conflicting sensor inputs (e.g., elevated heart rate paired with large kinetic variance), the model's calibrated posterior probability naturally settles within the uncertain margin $[1-\tau, \tau]$, triggering abstention without manual thresholding.

Operational coverage ($\mathcal{C}$) indicates the proportion of windows where the classifier outputs a definite prediction:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}(\hat{y}(x_i) \neq \text{Uncertain}) \times 100\%
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dual-Level LOSO Cross-Validation}
To assess generalization to unseen individuals, all evaluations follow a 15-fold Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) structure \cite{saeb2017need, little2019using}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level 1 (Outer LOSO Split):} In fold $k$, all data from the test subject are withheld for independent evaluation. The remaining 14 subjects form the training pool. Feature scaling (\texttt{StandardScaler}) is fit exclusively on the 14 training subjects.
    \item \textbf{Level 2 (Group-Aware Inner Calibration):} To prevent leakage across overlapping temporal windows, \texttt{CalibratedClassifierCV} uses an inner \texttt{GroupKFold(n\_splits=3)} grouped strictly by \texttt{subject\_id} across the training participants. This ensures that no individual's windows span both training and calibration folds.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Eight-Model Benchmark Suite (7 Tabular ML + 1 Deep MLP)}
To examine performance consistency across algorithms, we evaluate eight models:
1) \textit{Logistic Regression (LR)}: Standard linear baseline with L2 penalty;
2) \textit{Decision Tree (DT)}: CART tree (max\_depth=5);
3) \textit{k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN)}: Distance-based ($k=5$);
4) \textit{AdaBoost}: Adaptive boosting ($n_{\text{est}}=50$);
5) \textit{Random Forest (RF)}: Bagged ensemble ($n_{\text{est}}=100$, max\_depth=5);
6) \textit{LightGBM}: Gradient boosted trees ($n_{\text{est}}=100$, learning\_rate=0.08);
7) \textit{XGBoost}: Extreme gradient boosting ($n_{\text{est}}=100$, max\_depth=5, learning\_rate=0.08, subsample=0.8) \cite{chen2016xgboost};
8) \textit{MLP (Deep Learning)}: Multi-layer perceptron (Linear(25, 64) $\rightarrow$ BatchNorm $\rightarrow$ ReLU $\rightarrow$ Dropout(0.3) $\rightarrow$ Linear(64, 32) $\rightarrow$ ReLU $\rightarrow$ Linear(32, 2)), optimized via Adam ($lr=10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, 100 epochs with Early Stopping). The MLP provides a standardized parametric neural baseline evaluated on the identical 25-feature tabular input.

\subsection{Statistical Evaluation and Bootstrap Confidence Intervals}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test:} Two-sided non-parametric test over paired LOSO test folds, reporting test statistic $W$, exact $p$-value, and rank-biserial effect size ($r_{rb}$).
    \item \textbf{95\% Bootstrap Confidence Intervals:} Non-parametric cluster-adjusted bootstrapping ($B = 1000$ iterations) over out-of-fold predictions to compute intervals [$\text{CI}_{95}^{\text{low}}, \text{CI}_{95}^{\text{high}}$].
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Motion-Induced False Alarm Metric \texorpdfstring{($\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}}$)}{(FAR-motion)}}
We utilize $\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}}$ to quantify error rates specifically during physical movement. The movement threshold $\theta_{\text{motion}}$ is set at the 80th percentile of acceleration variance $\text{Var}(\text{ACC})$ across the corpus ($\theta_{\text{motion}} = 15.385$). The metric measures the proportion of non-stress windows with elevated motion that are incorrectly classified as stress:
\begin{equation}
\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}} = \frac{\sum_{i} \mathbb{I}(\hat{y}_i = 1,\, y_i = 0,\, \text{Var}_i \ge \theta_{\text{mot}})}{\sum_{i} \mathbb{I}(y_i = 0,\, \text{Var}_i \ge \theta_{\text{mot}})} \times 100\%
\end{equation}
where the denominator corresponds to all 295 high-motion non-stress windows in WESAD.

Results across the benchmark indicate two primary findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Broad Motion Vulnerability:} Under mandatory full coverage (100\%), all models exhibit elevated false alarm rates during physical activity: $\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}}$ ranges from $12.20\%$ (XGBoost) to $24.00\%$ (MLP). Although the MLP achieves competitive overall discrimination ($F_1 = 88.95\%$), it also records the highest motion false alarm rate ($24.00\%$). This observation confirms that motion vulnerability is primarily a physiological confound rather than an artifact of a specific algorithm family.
    \item \textbf{Impact of Selective Abstention:} Applying selective classification at $\tau = 0.80$ reduces motion false alarms across \textbf{all eight architectures} (relative reductions between 55.0\% and 90.0\%), while raising Macro $F_1$ by 3.6\% to 8.0\%. XGBoost achieves a balanced operating profile: limiting $\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}}$ to $4.07\%$ (a $66.6\%$ reduction relative to raw baseline) and achieving 95.20\% Selective Macro $F_1$ at 69.42\% coverage. XGBoost is selected as our primary operational architecture because it occupies the superior Pareto-optimal frontier across the multi-objective clinical triage spectrum—maximizing diagnostic discrimination while retaining over 6.2\% more usable monitoring windows.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Tabular ML (XGBoost) vs. Deep Learning (MLP)}
Statistical evaluation using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and cluster bootstrap differences across the 15 LOSO folds shows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full Coverage Comparison:} In unconstrained full coverage, Macro $F_1$ between MLP ($88.95\%$) and XGBoost ($88.13\%$) shows no statistically significant difference ($W = 14.0, p = 0.975, r_{rb} = -0.010, p_{\text{holm}} = 1.0$). However, XGBoost shows lower baseline motion susceptibility ($\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}} = 12.20\%$ vs. $24.00\%$ for MLP).
    \item \textbf{Selective Performance at $\tau = 0.80$:} Both models exceed 95\% Selective Macro $F_1$ ($95.38\%$ for MLP, $95.20\%$ for XGBoost). The 95\% paired bootstrap difference $(MLP - XGB)$ contains zero: $\Delta F_1 = +0.0117$ (95\% CI: $[-0.0256, +0.0513]$) and $\Delta \text{FAR}_{\text{sel}} = +2.03\%$ (95\% CI: $[-5.44\%, +8.54\%]$), indicating that selective abstention functions consistently across tabular and neural formulations.
    \item \textbf{Subject-Level Stability:} On Subject S14 (evaluated in Fold 12), the MLP captures subtle non-linear cues ($F_{1,\text{stress}} = 97.2\%$), whereas tree algorithms underperform due to suppressed EDA amplitude. Conversely, the MLP exhibits complete abstention on Subject S17 (Fold 15), yielding 0\% coverage. In contrast, XGBoost maintains stable coverage across all 15 subjects ($69.42\% \pm 22.2\%$).
    \item \textbf{Wearable Edge Systems Rationale:} From an embedded systems perspective, tree ensembles like XGBoost offer substantial practical advantages over deep end-to-end architectures. While raw-waveform temporal neural networks require intensive floating-point computation, large SRAM allocations, and significant battery drain, our domain-engineered feature extraction pipeline requires only 25 statistical features computed over sliding windows. This feature set can be executed on ultra-low-power microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M4/M33 operating under sub-milliwatt power budgets), enabling multi-day continuous ambulatory monitoring on standard wristwear battery capacities.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This study evaluated wrist-based stress detection under mandatory 100\% prediction versus selective classification. Across eight model families evaluated under 15-fold LOSO cross-validation, motion-induced false alarms proved widespread under forced prediction, whereas selective classification suppressed these false alerts by 55.0\% to 90.0\%. The proposed framework yields strong reliability metrics (Selective Macro $F_1 = 95.20\%$, ECE $= 0.0290$, $\text{FAR}_{\text{motion}} = 4.07\%$) while maintaining $69.42\%$ operational coverage. Supported by TreeSHAP interpretability and validation across 15 participants, the approach offers practical design principles for wearable health monitoring.
